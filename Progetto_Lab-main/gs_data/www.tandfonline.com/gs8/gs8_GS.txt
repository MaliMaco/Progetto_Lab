Haunted Histories of Experimental Psychology: A Re-Examination of Hodgson and Davey’s Mal-Observation Report
ABSTRACT
In 1887, psychical researchers Richard Hodgson and S. J. Davey published a study that revealed counterintuitive aspects of human cognition: How healthy, sober, honest observers of events can be prone to seemingly remarkable errors of eyewitness testimony. Their report, titled ‘The Possibilities of Mal-Observation and Lapse of Memory from a Practical Point of View’ included a collection of eyewitness statements from participants in a series of hoax séances. Hodgson and Davey asserted that all phenomena in their séances were produced using techniques drawn from performance magic. However, their participants were not initially informed about this fact. In their accounts, participants consistently attributed events that they saw, not to trickery, but to spiritual phenomena and psychic powers on the part of Davey, who played the role of the ‘medium’. A major advantage this novel paradigm was that the experimenters had a highly reliable record of actual events, because they had orchestrated the séances themselves. Their paper emphasised the contrasts between what their participants reported remembering relative to what had actually occurred. The errors of the witnesses were marked by failures to notice or remember key aspects of the séance, and even instances where the participants seemed to confabulate- confidently reporting events that had never really taken place. Neither Hodgson nor Davey would have identified themselves as psychologists or cognitive scientists, rather they considered themselves to be psychical researchers, operating under the auspices of the Society for Psychical Research (SPR). At the time, experimental psychology was only just beginning to emerge as a formal and distinct academic discipline. But looking back on their work from a modern perspective, it arguably anticipated more formal theoretical and empirical developments in cognitive psychology by decades. Ironically, given that their report systematically documented failures of attention and lapses of memory, it has been largely ignored in historical considerations of psychology research and is relatively unknown today. In this paper, I will explore the historical context of Hodgson and Davey’s report and discuss how some of their key findings can be related to our contemporary scientific understanding of attention, memory, metacognition, and the science of magic.
Introduction
In 1887, psychical researchers Richard Hodgson and S. J. Davey published a report detailing a series of experiments designed to examine the reliability of eyewitness testimony.Footnote1 Their article, ‘The Possibilities of Mal-observation and Lapse of Memory from a Practical Point of View’, (referred to throughout this paper as the Mal-Observation Report) describes how they conducted a series of performances using sleight-of-hand methods to apparently demonstrate supernatural communication with the spirits of the dead. Before explaining that the effects were tricks, they asked their participants to produce written accounts of what they remembered. These written accounts revealed that the participants reliably misperceived and misremembered key aspects of the performances, with many of them being unable to explain the real methods behind the trick and resorting to supernatural explanations. Not only is this one of the first recorded instances of studying participants’ reactions to sleight-of-hand magic tricks, but it is arguably one of the earliest experimental psychological accounts of phenomena now known as reconstructive memory and inattentional blindness, which Hodgson and Davey referred to at the time as mal-observation. Both reconstructive memory and inattentional blindness are concepts that did not receive formal labels or widespread scientific recognition until many years after Hodgson and Davey’s report. Today, the term ‘reconstructive memory’ refers to the concept that our ability to recall past events involves an active reconstruction based on our general knowledge, assumptions, and expectations, as opposed to simply being a faithful reproduction of past experiences.Footnote2 A reconstructive memory process can result in accurate memories, but a direct consequence of this reconstructive process is that it is prone to errors and false recollections. Traditionally, psychologists attribute the initial theoretical conceptualisation of reconstructive memory to the work of Fredrick Bartlett, a psychologist who is credited with pioneering formal investigations on human memory outside of laboratory settings.Footnote3 Subsequently, another key body of experimental and theoretical literature on reconstructive memory is attributed to the works of Elizabeth Loftus, which, since the 1970s, empirically demonstrated that reconstructive processes can yield significant and long-lasting changes to reported memories, including the generation of ‘false memories’ – elaborate recollections for events that never actually occurred but are phenomenologically indistinguishable from ‘true’ memories of events that genuinely occurred.Footnote4 Another phenomenon that would not be formally recognised until decades later, but is nonetheless evident in Hodgson and Davey’s report, is ‘inattentional blindness’. This term describes the fact that we can fail to detect visual objects or events that are fully visible and in plain sight when our attention is otherwise engaged. The term first emerged in the 1990s, when psychologists Mack and Rock reported a series of experiments, wherein they used simple computerised visual displays to demonstrate that participants would often fail to notice obvious visual stimuli.Footnote5 Later studies have demonstrated that inattentional blindness is not limited to failures to notice simple shapes on a computer monitor, but can also occur in more naturalistic, complex, and dynamic scenes.Footnote6 Both reconstructive memory and inattentional blindness are deeply counterintuitive psychological concepts that seem to contradict everyday human experience.Footnote7 It feels impossible that we can generate real-seeming memories of events that never happened, or that we can fail to see seemingly obvious visual events that occur right before our eyes. Such disconnections between how people feel cognition works, and how it actually functions can be classified as metacognitive illusions. Thanks to a large body of careful empirical work that accumulated throughout the twentieth century, inattentional blindness and reconstructive memory are now firmly established to be a natural part of healthy everyday human cognitive processes. But despite the fact that the Hodgson and Davey’s 1887 report systematically described many of these phenomena, it is generally overlooked, aside from rather specialised publications related to magic illusions, anomalistic psychology, or parapsychology.Footnote8 According to Google Scholar (accessed 14 August 2025), Hodgson and Davey’s report has been cited a total of only 70 times. In contrast, Fredric Bartlett’s Remembering has been cited 28,682 times, Elizabeth Loftus’ most cited review chapter related to false memory formation has been cited 5218 times, and Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris’ iconic study of inattentional blindness has been cited 4691 times. Footnote9 In this paper, I aim to make the case that Hodgson and Davey’s Mal-Observation Report is well worth remembering and attending.

Setting the Stage
The Mal-Observation Report was published at a confluence of three historically parallel trends: The rise of ‘Modern Spiritualism’, the popularity of performance magic as entertainment, and the emergence of experimental psychology as a formal scientific and academic discipline.Footnote10 Modern Spiritualism refers to a socio-cultural religious movement that spread throughout the US and around the world, starting in the mid-1800s. Many religious practices promote the idea that a person’s spirit or soul survives beyond death, but spiritualism set itself apart from more conventional religions in that proponents asserted that the spirits of the dead could physically interact and directly communicate with the living. Furthermore, the miracles of Modern Spiritualism were not relegated to stories of ancient texts, rather they were presented as contemporary, participatory, and, above all, empirical. Anyone could personally witness proof of spiritualistic phenomena; no faith was required.

As spiritualistic practices spread and flourished in popularity, spiritualistic phenomena allegedly manifested in variety of ways. Early mediums, like the Fox Sisters initially claimed communicated with spirits through ‘raps’ or knocking noises.Footnote11 But alternative and more spectacular methods of demonstrating spiritual phenomena rapidly proliferated. In the most extreme instances of physical phenomena, spirits were said to visibly manifest in plain view as disembodied faces, hands, or full bodies. Or the spirits might invisibly hoist objects and musical instruments. Spiritualist mediums would claim to ‘channel’ spirits that could provide them with extraordinary abilities, such as access to knowledge that they had no earthly way of knowing or allow them to perform physically impossible feats like levitation.Footnote12 New modalities of spirit communication likewise emerged. Such methods included table tilting, wherein invisible spirits would appear to shake small tables. In other instances, the spirits might engage in direct voice communication wherein they would speak through the voices of the medium. Sometimes the spirits would cause levitations of objects or even the mediums themselves. Of particular relevance to the present discussion was the practice of ‘psychography’ or independent slate-writing.Footnote13 In slate-writing séances, the medium would present participants with one or more school slates — small handheld blackboards that were of common use by schoolchildren at that time (Figure 1). The slates would be shown to be free of any marks. And then, under circumstances where it would seem that no one could have physically written on the slates, writing would appear. Such writings were attributed to spirit agency.

Several slate-writing mediums achieved celebrity status, making them of particular interest to both the general public and the scientific community. One of the most notorious slate-writing mediums was Dr. Henry Slade. Slade was an American spirit medium who began his career as a ‘clairvoyant physician’ (his title of doctor was not attributable to any academic or medical institution), but he would go on to become internationally famous for his propensity for producing slate-written communications from spirits.Footnote15 This skillset led him to travel extensively around the world. While visiting London in 1877, he was arrested and tried for fraud.Footnote16 The trial drew international attention and ended with Slade being convicted of fraud, but then having his conviction overturned on a legal technicality. He fled Britain before he could be re-tried, eventually ending up in Leipzig Germany, where he demonstrated his powers to luminaries from the field psychophysics (a precursor of experimental psychology), including Gustav Fechner, Johann Zöllner, the university’s chair of astrophysics, and Wilhelm Wundt, who would later be dubbed the founding father of experimental psychology.Footnote17 Over the course of multiple sittings over several weeks, Slade not only demonstrated slate-writing but a variety of phenomena that were (seemingly) unexplainable by conventional physical laws, including the deflection of a compass needle with his mind, tying impossible knots in string, materialising and dematerialising small objects, clairvoyance, and even the manifestation of a tangible spirit hand. Zöllner was impressed with Slade’s abilities, so much so that he proposed a new scientific discipline of Transcendentale Physik [Transcendental Physics] to explain Slade’s mediumship in terms of fourth dimensional geometry.Footnote18 Zöllner rushed to publish his findings and theories as a multi-volume set of Wissenschaftliche Abhandlungen (‘Scientific Papers’ published from 1877–1878), which contained more than 2000 pages describing his investigations of Slade. The writings stirred controversy not only in Germany but around the world.Footnote19 In 1880, Charles Carleton Massesy, a British barrister, compiled and translated selections of Zöllner’s German writings and published them under the title Transcendental Physics—the book proved to be popular, particularly with English-speaking spiritualists, and was republished in multiple editions. Footnote20 Crucially, Zöllner was aware of accusations that Slade was dishonest, but he asserted that Slade was an honest man who was being unfairly slandered by small-minded people.Footnote21 One such person was Wilhelm Wundt, who had been invited to witness one of Slade’s demonstrations, published an open letter asserting that Slade’s demonstrations could be more rationally dismissed as ‘jugglery’ or sleight-of-hand magic methods.Footnote22 However, despite rhetorically gesturing towards the idea of magic trick methods, Wundt, as a non-magician himself, did not offer any substantive methodological explanations for how Slade might have accomplished such convincing tricks. Zöllner continued to promote Transcendental Physics until his death in 1882, while Wundt went on to establish the world’s first-ever formally recognised laboratory of experimental psychology at Leipzig in 1883.Footnote23

In developing his lab and legacy, Wundt never returned to investigations of magic and spiritual fraud. Arguably, his and other psychologists’ disengagement from psychical research represented a deliberate strategic effort to distance the nascent field of experimental psychology from associations with spiritual phenomena.Footnote24 But other researchers were more eager to explore both magic tricks and the scientific claims associated with the modern spiritualism movement. In 1882, a group of scholars in Cambridge banded together to form the Society for Psychical Research (SPR), with the goal of investigating phenomena associated with the spiritualist movement. The membership came to include scientific luminaries like Alfred Russell Wallace, William Crookes, William James, and Oliver Lodge. The SPR was specifically established to engage with spiritualist claims, including mediumship, apparitional experiences, haunted houses, and the survival of the soul after bodily death. The SPR’s main mechanism for disseminating its findings was through printed publications. The society produced a monthly Journal of the Society for Psychical Research that was intended exclusively to members, and they also produced a public facing series of Proceedings of the Society of Psychical Research, which were intended for public consumption. The exact nature of the SPR’s distribution numbers in the 1880s are not explicitly documented in contemporary publications of their Journal or Proceedings. However, in a recorded speech given in 1885 by the SPR’s then president, Balfour Stewart, he remarked that:

In reply to the question, what has the Society done? I may state that since its commencement it has issued seven parts of Proceedings, of which a total number exceeding 12,000 has been distributed to Members and others, placed in public libraries, sent for review, and sold through the ordinary channels.Footnote25

Advertisements for the SPR’s Proceedings could be found in regional newspapers. For example, the issue of the Proceedings featuring the Mal-Observation Report could be ordered by anyone for the price of 4 shillingsFootnote26 (Figure 2), about 25 pounds or 29 euros in today’s currencyFootnote27 (Bank of England, Citation2025). The SPR members’ methods of communicating their findings and discussions through printed media, both internally and externally, arguably reveal important patterns in the ways that researchers have presented debates about scientific developments. The impacts of the practices were not limited to the domain of paranormal studies: Lamont identifies these scientific debates around spiritualism as a Victorian crisis of evidence that would directly shape the development of modern conventions of academic psychology.Footnote28 Similarly, Efram Sera-Shriar has identified the SPR’s debates around credible witnessing – the question of who has the authority to make qualified observations of extraordinary phenomena — as re-shaping academic conventions of anthropology. Footnote29 Furthermore, Noakes has demonstrated how such debates and investigations influenced the modern practice of physics. Footnote30

Partially as a result of Henry Slade’s notoriety, the SPR became particularly concerned with the practice of psychography, and the value of evidence obtained by testimonies of this extraordinary phenomenon. Hodgson and Davey’s Mal-Observation Report was largely inspired by controversies related to eyewitness accounts of slate-writing séances. They were concerned, not only with accounts of Slade’s feats, but also with the alleged phenomena of William Eglinton,Footnote32 a British medium who developed his own reputation for slate-writing after Slade had fled England following his trial for fraud. The SPR received and published numerous accounts of extraordinary test sittings from various members of the public who had witnessed Eglinton in action.Footnote33 Eglinton would use slates that had been provided by the witness, and having placed a small piece of chalk between two blank slates, would produce messages. These were words selected by the participants, sometimes seemingly randomly chosen books that Eglinton had apparently no way of seeing. Frank Podmore, one of the SPR’s founding members wrote, in 1902, that ‘In the history of the movement, no physical manifestation had ever won such universal recognition’ as Eglinton’s abilities.Footnote34 Accounts of Eglinton’s mediumship sparked a fiery debate in the pages of the SPR’s publications. Eleanor Mildred Sidgwick published a sceptical paper cautioning that the Society should never seriously consider accounts of mediums who have ever been credibly accused of fraud, and she specifically accused Eglinton of having been caught faking manifestations. She wrote that he had been caught with props, including muslin drapes and a false beard that were clearly intended to be used for creating hoax apparitions. Sidgwick attributed the remarkable accounts of his activities to a combination of conjuring (i.e., magic trick) methods being deployed by the medium and ‘mal-observation’ on the part of witnesses (i.e., perceptual and memory illusions).Footnote35 Her sceptical assertions were met with a scepticism on the part of Eglinton’s supporters. Charles Carleton Massey (who had produced the English translations of Zöllner’s investigations of Henry Slade) directly responded to Sidgwick by asserting that mal-observation could not possibly account for the phenomena allegedly produced by Eglinton. ‘Most of us would feel ashamed’, he wrote, ‘of mistaking a conjurer’s trick for a genuine manifestation of unknown force’.Footnote36 Magicians, he wrote, could only accomplish their deceptions under conditions that were highly inferior to that of a test medium. According to Massey, in séances, participants generally knew exactly what phenomena were going to be produced. Magicians, he argued, would be unable to successfully perform illusion if their audiences were aware of exactly what tricks they would be performing; such uncertainty ‘does not and cannot exist’.Footnote37 If deception was possible for Eglinton during his slate-writing séances, Massey argued that the only reasonable conclusion would be that all accounts based on human observation would have to be considered ‘absolutely worthless’.Footnote38 He appealed to his readers to consider that the witnesses to Eglinton (and other mediums) could not be so easily fooled. While he acknowledged that an honest witness might sometimes forget certain facts and events, he argued that they could never honestly ‘invent specific and positive events of perception’.Footnote39 Massey’s sentiments were representative of other scientists who espoused the validity and importance of spiritualistic phenomena. For example, Alfred Russell Wallace, a scientific luminary in the field of biology, known for co-developing the theory of natural selection along with Charles Darwin, had written that the miracles of spiritualism had ‘been tested and examined by sceptics of every grade of incredulity, men in every way qualified to detect imposture or discover natural causes- trained physicists, medical men, lawyers, and men of business- but in every case the investigators have retired baffled, or become converts’.Footnote40

In response to this line of argumentation, Sidgwick turned to Angelo John Lewis, a barrister and a semi-professional magician who performed and wrote under the stage name Professor Hoffmann. Lewis not only examined written accounts by Eglinton’s witnesses, but also personally attended twelve sittings with Eglinton. In describing his analysis of the witness accounts of Eglinton’s phenomenaFootnote41,Footnote42, Lewis was careful to acknowledge that he was open to the possibility of genuine spiritual phenomena, but wrote that in his opinion as a magician, the witness accounts were indeed suggestive of errors of observation that might be attributable to conjuring deceptions. He noted that witnesses to magic performances did not necessarily describe events that they saw, but rather events that they imagined they saw. He also acknowledged that even highly trained magicians could still be fooled by conjuring methods that they were not personally familiar with or not expecting. As to Lewis’ personal experiences sitting with Eglinton, it transpired that throughout repeated attempts ‘the spirits obstinately declined to manifest’. Such blank séances presented the debating researchers with something of a rhetorical stalemate. Proponents of spiritualism could argue that the uneventful sittings were evidence in favour of the genuineness of mediums, after all, if the medium was resorting to conjuring then, surely, they would always be able to perform their tricks. And thus, the unreliability of their mediumship supported the idea that reported spiritual phenomena were logically attributable to forces outside of the mediums’ direct control. On the other hand, sceptics could argue that fraudulent mediums would deliberately avoid using trickery if they suspected they were being observed by a sitter with conjuring expertise (as Lewis was well known for). And the medium could simply wait to perform their deceptions until they were in the presence of a more gullible audience, while claiming that the blank séances were outside their control.

Hodgson and Davey’s Mal-Observation Report
Hodgson and Davey’s Mal-Observation Report describes a scientific investigation that they developed as a way of around the empirical problem of investigating the reliability of testimonies related allegedly genuine spiritualist mediumship. By using magic trick methods to simulate slate-writing phenomena, they avoided the problem of blank séances that had plagued Lewis’ investigation. Furthermore, by relying exclusively on magic trick methods performed by an openly fake medium who made no claim to supernormal abilities, they (vainly) hoped to avoid the potential criticism that any genuine psychical phenomena might occur during their experiments. Hodgson took care to declare that even though the participants testimonies might seem ‘marvellous enough’ to support the hypothesis of occult agency, ‘the “psychographic” phenomena described in the following records are conjuring, and only conjuring, performances’.Footnote43 ‘The object of the notes’, he wrote, was not to educate readers about particular conjuring methods, but ‘to show to investigators the kind and degree of mistakes which may be made by educated and intelligent witnesses in recording their impression of a performance the main lines of which are planned with the deliberate intention of deceiving them, but few, if any, of the details of which can be described as absolutely fixed’.Footnote44

The Mal-Observation Report represents the synthesis of Hodgson’s first-hand experience debunking professional mediums and Davey’s conjuring expertise. Richard Hodgson (Figure 3) was born in Melbourne, Australia and obtained a degree in Law from Cambridge University.Footnote46 During his time at Cambridge, Hodgson joined the recently formed SPR. As a member of the society, Hodgson had developed reputation as a talented detective debunker of spiritualist mediums. He spearheaded one of the SPR’s first major investigations into spiritualism when he travelled to India to investigate Countess Helena Petrova de Blavatsky, a Russian occultist who had attracted a significant following based on her alleged supernatural abilities. As a founder of the Theosophy movement, Blavatsky claimed that she was directly channelling the spiritual teachings of dead mystics to develop what has variously been described as an occult or new religious system.Footnote47 Hodgson spent two months in India attempting to verify Blavatsky’s claims. In his eventual report, which he published through the SPR in 1885, he declared that the phenomena that she claimed credit for were entirely fraudulent.Footnote48 According to Hodgson, the letters Blavatsky claimed to receive from spirits of the dead were written in her own hand, and the spirit forms she manifested were simply human accomplices wearing disguises. Hodgson asserted that Blavatsky’s followers were ‘excessively credulous and deficient in observation’, and that she was actually the centre of a ‘huge fraudulent system’.Footnote49 Hodgson’s experiences investigating Blavatsky arguably helped spark his initial interest in the relationship between magic tricks and eyewitness testimony, and after meeting S. J. Davey, he would go on to co-develop the mal-observation paradigm to put their ideas to an empirical test.

Hodgson’s co-experimenter, Samuel John Davey (Figure 4), was an amateur magician. Before he began practising magic, Davey had been a true believer in the reality of spiritualist phenomena. He wrote that he initially came to his beliefs after seeing a recently deceased friend in a dream, and that his beliefs were further inspired by reading an English translation of Zöllner’s Transcendental Physics.Footnote50 He also personally witnessed the apparently miraculous talents of the medium William Eglinton. Davey was particularly fascinated by the phenomenon of slate-writing. In the Mal-Observation Report, Davey confessed that, for a time, he actually came to believe that he himself possessed genuine supernatural talents. He experimented in trying to receive messages from spirits by leaving blank slates in locked drawers in his house. He was astonished upon examining some of the slates to find that messages had appeared on the slates that he himself had cleaned and locked away. Eventually, he came to realise that his friends were hoaxing him by secretly marking the slates themselves through entirely natural means. This experience of being tricked led Davey to begin studying conjuring. And, consequently, he came to appreciate that many of the effects that he had previously considered to be miraculous were actually attributable to magic trick methods. Davey soon progressed beyond academic curiosity about magic and began putting his conjuring knowledge into practice. He soon came to appreciate that the errors of observation that witnesses made of his own performances might very well parallel the kinds of errors he now thought that he had once made when observing mediums:

I noticed that many persons made statements concerning my performances, as to the conditions of the production of the writing, which were just as emphatic as I made in my own reports about Eglinton, and I also noticed that nearly all these statements were entirely wrong. Even when I sometimes revealed the fact that I was merely a conjurer, the reply which I frequently received was something of this kind: ‘Yes, you may say it is conjuring, but it could not have been done by that means when I did so-and-so’ (describing a supposed test) ‘and yet we got the writing all the same’.Footnote51

Davey approached the Society for Psychical Research to demonstrate his developing conjuring skills. And after witnessing Davey’s tricks, Richard Hodgson and Eleanor Sidgwick realised that Davey’s pseudo-mediumship represented an opportunity for them to develop a new method to empirically investigate testimonies of alleged psychic phenomena.

The Mal-Observation Report opens with a general introduction written by Hodgson describing his own experiences investigating psychic fraud. He professed having once been very confident that eyewitness testimonies of séances represented examples of genuine phenomena. However, over time, as he learned more about the possibilities of trickery and conjuring methods, he grew more and more sceptical. Hodgson directly addresses both Alfred Russell Wallace’s and Charles Carleton Massey’s assertions that testimonies of personal experiences could be used to definitively establish the empirical reality of spiritualism. Footnote53 He described how his personal experiences of seeing witnesses being fooled by illusions, combined with his own empirical investigations with Davey, suggested that human testimony, particularly in the context magic performances, was much less reliable than past researchers had appreciated. The second part of the Mal-Observation Report, the Experimental Investigation, was authored by Davey. He began by briefly describing his own personal journey from credulity to scepticism before presenting a series of written statements from the participants, accompanied by his own meta-commentary. Over the course of several months, Hodgson and Davey had invited various members of the public to attend séances hosted by Davey. Most, but not all the séances took place in Davey’s home. The séances were loosely scripted and would vary between different audiences as Davey improvised around individual circumstances and reactions. However, at the start of each performance, he would always specifically caution his audience to be mindful of any form of trickery or deception. However, he would not specifically tell them that he was going to be presenting magic tricks, nor did he ever explicitly attribute his performance to supernatural powers. At the conclusion of each demonstration, audience members were asked to provide written accounts in the form of letters, describing everything that they had experienced. Hodgson explained the motivation for their experiments by making direct reference to the debate between Sidgwick and Massey:

It appeared that a large number of the readers of Mrs. Sidgwick’s article in the Journal for June were prejudiced in favour of ordinary’ human powers of observation and recollection under - it is to be remembered -exceptionally adverse circumstances; and that they were thus prejudiced simply because they had never made any special experiments, with the view of ascertaining exactly how much reliance could be placed upon the reports of even acute and intelligent observers of the ‘slate-writing’ performances of a conjurer known as such. They had decided a priori as to the capacity of human perception and memory under quite peculiar conditions, and most of them, I venture to say, had thus decided, not only without possessing any familiarity with the various modes of producing’ slate-writing’ by conjuring, but without possessing any familiarity with conjuring tricks in general, and without being aware of the extent to which we are all subject to illusions of Memory, which, in relation to the reports of ‘psychography’, are more deserving of consideration than even illusions of Perception.Footnote54

In total, the Mal-Observation Report collected 27 distinct accounts from 17 separate performances (Figure 5). Each witness account was reproduced in full and was accompanied by commentary from Hodgson and Davey about the ways that the participants’ reports deviated from the actual events. Participants generally endorsed Davey as a genuine medium, and they made references to the inexplicability of his performances. For example, part of an account from one Mrs. M. Johnson, written in September 1886, reads as follows:

My sisters and I being most interested in the subject of slate-writing and anxious to see something of it, Mr. Davey kindly arranged a meeting at his house. We sat at an ordinary table in a well-lighted room, and writing was quickly produced on the inner surface of one of two slates held firmly together, once by Mr. Davey and myself, at other times by my sister and Mr. Davey; at first just under the edge of the table, then above, and afterwards on one of my sister’s shoulders. This was the more wonderful as we had purchased the slates on our way from the station. Of course between the slates were placed three points of different coloured chalks, after which Mr. Davey asked us in which colour the writing should appear, and it did so in the colour we elected, the slate being covered with writing. We are all quite certain that the slates were never out of the hands of one or other of us, and we are totally unable to account for the slate-writing.Footnote55

Another account by Mr. John H. Rait, concerning a sitting that took place on the 8 September 1896, described in detail how Mr. Davey had inexplicably produced writing between slates that had been securely locked together. Mr. Rait concluded that: ‘Trickery to my mind is utterly impossible in any respect. How it is all done I cannot tell; my advice to the “sceptics” is “go and judge for yourselves”’.Footnote56 Likewise, an account written by a ‘Mrs. Y’ of sitting that occurred on 10 September 1896 reports how Davey produced writing on a clean slate while she carefully watched his hands for any sign of movement, and how he also produced writing between two locked slates that matched a line from a random page in a book that she had freely selected from a shelf in the room. And how he had caused small fragments of chalk, isolated under a clear tumbler to apparently animate in front of her eyes. She finished her account by stating: ‘I consider the test conditions to have been perfect throughout, and see no possible explanation for the very remarkable phenomena that occurred’.Footnote57

Overall, the participants committed consistent and systematic errors in their testimonies: The reports typically omitted crucial elements of the séances — specifically elements that were instrumental for the accomplishment of the conjuring tricks. For example, in one of the scripted segments of the séance Davey would surreptitiously switch a blank slate for another slate that had been pre-marked with writing before the experiment began. Davey conducted most of his performances while seated at a table. He would begin by showing the blank slate clearly; he would then place the slate under the table, where he would switch it for the slate that had writing on it that had previously been concealed beneath the table. He would then remove the slate with writing on it under the table and leave the genuinely blank slate behind. To help conceal the switch, he would not reveal the writing immediately. Instead, he would pretend that the slate was still blank, and he would explicitly express his disappointment that no spirits had been in contact. This ruse was accepted by his audiences, who remembered examining the original slate. Next, Davey would place the pre-written slate on top the table (writing side down) and ask the sitter(s) to place their hands upon it. Davey would then wait for a brief time and then ask them to turn over the slate. At this point, the writing was revealed to the sitters. He would then pretend that the writing had just manifested while the participants’ hands were on the slate. The trick was effective because the participants failed to recognise the significance of the moment when Davey placed the slates under the table, and they consistently failed to record this moment in their accounts of the séance. Thus, participants’ written accounts accurately reflected their experience of seeing writing appear on the slate under seemingly impossible conditions, but they also failed to accurately reflect the reality of what had actually happened. As a result of these omissions, even a reader well versed in fraudulent slate-writing methods would struggle to provide an explanation of how the writing had appeared.

The Mal-Observation Report’s documentation of witnesses’ omissions is very similar to the idea of inattentional blindnessFootnote58 - a psychological phenomenon that was not formally identified until over a hundred years after the Mal-Observation Report. Davey’s actions of placing the slate beneath the table were clearly visible and in plain sight of the participants. Yet their belief that the writing was produced while they held the slates and their omission of the action of placing the slate under the table indicates that participants nonetheless failed to detect these significant actions. The fact that participants were instructed to watch his actions closely, and clearly believed they had done so, suggests that, at least in some instances, their failures to detect the secret methods may have been related to having their attention otherwise engaged. However, given the fact that the reports were collected some time after the critical events, it is not possible to definitively distinguish between failures of awareness that might have occurred during the performance and subsequent failures of memory that might have occurred after the performance.

Beyond documenting omissions in the participants’ testimonies, the report also demonstrated that participants engaged in ‘positive’ errors; they confabulated some events that were the products of their own imaginations. For example, one of the participants, Mr. Padshah, described his experience in Sitting IV of the experiment.Footnote59 According to Padshah, the most remarkable part of his experience was when the written image of his childhood nickname appeared unexpectedly on a slate: ‘ … On the large slate, instead of the numbers we wanted, we got written “Boorzu”. Now this as it happens is the original Persian, the modern corruption of which is my initial name’.Footnote60This was particularly impressive to Padshah, because he had not been called this name in many years, and, to his knowledge, no one else present in the room was aware that he had ever been called that. In a note, Hodgson explained exactly how this had actually happened was that Davey had secretly written the word ‘BOOKS’ on the slate to suggest a further test (i.e., a prepared trick) involving books in the room. But when Padshah saw the scrawled word, he mis-read it as ‘BOORZU’, and this minor proofreading error led him to the extraordinary conclusion that a spiritual entity was addressing him personally by a name that no one else in the room, including the medium, could possibly have known by natural means. Davey quickly improvised by wiping the word away before Padshah or anyone else could get a clear look at it, and the other participants were highly impressed by Padshah’s dramatic reaction. One Mr. Russell, who had also been attending the same sitting as a witness, noted in his report:

Mr. D. now said that a start having been made, more success might be looked for, so the experiment was repeated, the slate being brought up at interval of from 5 to 10 minutes. The first time it had the letters BOORZ written upon it, which Mr. P. explained were the first five letters of his Christian name which was in Persian written BOORZU. Neither Mr. Hodgson, Mr. Hughes, my wife, nor myself had ever heard of this name before.Footnote61

In general, contemporary researchers and commentators including the Mal-Observation Reports authors, seemed open to the idea that errors of omission, i.e., forgetfulness and inattention, were fairly commonplace; however, the idea that honest, healthy, sober, intelligent witnesses could form reports based on memories of falsely remembered events was highly controversial. Yet the fact that people can and do create false memories of imagined events that can feel subjectively indistinguishable from ‘real’ memories of actual events is exactly what the evidence from Hodgson and Davey’s study indicated, and this idea has been borne out by subsequent experimental psychological research that took place many years afterwards.Footnote62

Initial Responses to the Report and the Follow-Up Article Regarding Davey’s Methods
Reactions to the Mal-Observation Report, following its publication, also indicate robust failures of metacognition in some of its readers. A lively written debate was recorded in the SPR’s Journal throughout the following years. One of the writers who actively engaged in the discussion around the Mal-Observation Report was Alfred Russell Wallace, who was, at the time, something of a scientific celebrity, having recently co-discovered the theory of evolution with Charles Darwin.Footnote63 While Wallace did not personally attend any of Davey’s séances, he attended similar slate-writing demonstrations by other mediums including Henry Slate and William Eglinton. Wallace was certain that he had personally witnessed genuine supernatural phenomena in the presence of various mediums. Even after Hodgson and Davey had explicitly written that they had been using magic trick methods, Wallace remained convinced that the séances conducted by Davey could potentially have involved genuine spiritual phenomena. He wrote an angry letter to the SPR suggesting that Hodgson and Davey were engaged in a deliberate cover-up of Davey’s mediumistic talents. Wallace took particular issue with the fact that the original report did not provide full methodological explanations of the conjuring techniques that were used to accomplish the simulated psychography phenomena. He went so far as to state that ‘unless all [the reported phenomena] can be so explained many of us will be confirmed in our belief that Mr. Davey was really a medium as well as a conjurer, and that in imputing all his performances to “trick” he was deceiving the Society and the public’.Footnote64 Wallace’s critique neatly illustrates the unfortunate potential consequences of actively employing deception as a tool to demonstrate how powerful deceptive methods can be. Similar issues have occurred throughout history, when magicians have attempted to use performance magic to promote scepticism of extraordinary claims. Arthur Conan Doyle, a committed spiritualist, famously maintained that his friend, the magician Harry Houdini, had accomplished his iconic escapes using psychic powers; this was all in spite of Houdini’s explicit disavowal and active debunking of psychic phenomena.Footnote65 Similar issues have continued to haunt those who use magic methods and deceptions as tools to explore belief in paranormal phenomena. In the 1980s, the magician James Randi orchestrated an elaborate hoax to publicly discredit the scientific rigour of contemporary parapsychological researchers. Randi’s methods involved having magicians infiltrate a lab posing as test subjects.Footnote66 In a carefully written critique of Randi’s efforts, sociologist (and magician) Marcello Truzzi actually drew explicit parallels to Hodgson and Davey’s report to help illuminate the issues around using fraudulent methods in efforts to expose fraud, noting that, logically, ‘ An irony in such cases is that once the defrauders admit to their fraud, the door is open to distrust them entirely’.Footnote67 More recently, in 2019, David Baker reported in Wired magazine about his experiences observing a psychology experiment where research students were instructed to assess the abilities of an allegedly psychic medium.Footnote68 The experiment was designed to explore how the participants formed beliefs in response to falsely presented empirical evidence.Footnote69 At the conclusion of the experiment session, the researchers conducted a ‘dehoaxing’ procedure wherein they explicitly revealed that the ‘psychic’ was actually a semi-professional magician (also the author of this paper) who had been using store-bought magic tricks to create illusions of paranormal phenomena. However, when Baker interviewed participants after this dehoaxing, some of them still maintained that what they saw could have been a demonstration of genuine psychic abilities: ‘I still think there was something weird about that guy. He could read minds’.Footnote70

Davey personally resisted fully revealing details of his trick methods.Footnote71 According to Hodgson, Davey worried that revealing specific methods might potentially lead other investigators to become overly confident when attempting to debunk professional mediums. And he was further concerned that, if people interested in committing future frauds read about his methods, they would then be able to adapt their own performances to be even more deceptive. He also wished to continue conducting more eyewitness experiments using the same methods that had been used in the Mal-Observation Report. Sadly, Davey did not have the opportunity to publish further research. He died of typhoid in 1891 at the age of 27.Footnote72 Shortly after Davey’s death, Hodgson acceded to Wallace and other commentators’ demands and published a follow-up article, titled Mr. Davey’s Imitations by Conjuring of Phenomena Sometimes Attributed to Spirit Agency.Footnote73 Throughout his paper, Hodgson provided many additional details about the methods that Davey had used in the séances, including the choreography of the slate switching, the use of ‘invisible threads’ to seemingly animate bits of chalk, and the fact that Davey had used a small ‘thimble writer’ (a device designed secretly to affix to his finger, see Figure 6) to surreptitiously mark the slates during his performances.

Connections to Subsequent Academic Research
As experimental psychology developed as a formal academic discipline, researchers have made remarkable progress in refining our scientific understanding of the cognitive mechanisms that underpin human attention and memory. Much subsequent published research has served to reinforce even the most controversial findings reported by Hodgson and Davey back in 1887. Their results anticipate the formal emergence of several concepts, including inattentional blindness and reconstructive memory. And they also broadly pre-empt developments related to the discipline of anomalistic psychology and modern psychological interest in the science of magic. This is despite the fact that the Mal-Observation Report itself has been largely unattended and forgotten by later researchers.

Inattentional Blindness
For readers unfamiliar with the term ‘inattentional blindness’ and who may not have seen a demonstration of the now classic basketball game paradigm, I highly recommend that you take a moment to watch the short (1 minute and 22 seconds) video before reading on. The clip is publicly available online (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vJG698U2Mvo), having been uploaded on YouTube by one of the original experimenters, Daniel Simons, under the title ‘Selective Attention Test’ on 10 March 2010 (see footnote for an archived stable link).Footnote77 The experiment from which the clip originates will be described in detail below, but actually watching the clip is one of the best ways of understanding the phenomena of inattentional blindness.

The concept of inattentional blindness was not formally established in psychological literature until the 1990s, when psychologists Irving Mack and Arian Rock published a book containing descriptions of a landmark series of experiments about how participants could fail to notice seemingly obvious stimuli that were fully visible and in plain sight when their attention was otherwise engaged.Footnote78 The book describes dozens of experiments that used simple computerised displays on monitors, conducted over the course of seven years, and involving hundreds of participants. In each experiment, participants would be explicitly instructed to engage in a simple ‘primary task’; for example, a cross-arm discrimination task (see Figure 7), where they viewed a brief presentation of cross and were asked to indicate whether the horizontal or vertical lines were longer.

Figure 7. Schematic of Mack and Rock’s computerized ‘cross-arm discrimination’ task.Footnote79 the top row represents a non-critical trial, where participants were instructed indicate whether the vertical or horizontal arms of the cross were longer. The second row represents a ‘critical trial’, participants received the same instructions, but in this case, an unexpected stimulus, a simple geometric shape, appeared alongside the cross. Participants were considered to be ‘inattentionally blind’ to the unexpected stimulus if they failed to report seeing it upon being questioned as to whether they noticed anything in addition to the cross being visible on the computer monitor.

Unbeknown to participants, on certain trials an additional critical stimulus was introduced that appeared alongside the primary task stimuli on screen. The key measure of these studies was whether participants would be able to report any awareness of this critical stimulus when questioned immediately after the trial. In many cases, the participants’ responses indicated that they were entirely unaware of the critical stimulus, such participants were considered to be inattentionally blind. No only did Mack and Rock’s series of experiments provide firm evidence that people can fail to detect stimuli that are plainly visible when their attention is otherwise engaged, but their methodology also provided a conceptual framework for investigating and describing these failures of visual awareness.Footnote80 This framework set the stage for what is perhaps the most dramatic empirical examples of inattentional blindness in scientific literature: Simons and Chabris’ Invisible Gorilla paradigm.Footnote81 This experiment adapted Mack and Rock’s framework and applied it to a more naturalistic and dynamic stimulus set. Rather than using simple static geometric shapes that would appear on a computer monitor, their experiment involved showing participants a live-action video recording. The stimulus video depicts two ‘teams’ of players, one team is dressed in white shirts, and the other in black shirts. Each team has a basketball that they pass only to their respective teammates. For their primary task, rather than discriminating which arm of a cross was longer, participants were instructed to count the number of times players passed a basketball. The ‘game’ lasts for a total of 24 seconds, and the white team makes a total of 15 passes. The critical stimulus, rather than being a simple shape on the monitor, was a woman wearing a full-body gorilla costume, who appears on screen about 10 seconds into the video. She enters from the right side of the video frame, strolls amongst the other players, pauses briefly in the centre of the action, beats her chest, and then continues strolling off the screen. The woman in the gorilla costume is fully visible for 10 seconds. Nonetheless, when people are focusing on the basketball passes, over half of them fail to notice the gorilla. Inattentional blindness is a robustly replicable phenomenon. Multiple studies have demonstrated that this phenomenon can apply to various stimuli including simple geometric shapes, to airplanes, motorcycles, unicycling clowns, fire extinguishers, simulated assaults, and weapons.Footnote82 But it is also deeply counterintuitive.Footnote83 In surveys of the general population, the majority of respondents tend to endorse such statements as: ‘Human memory works like a video camera, accurately recording the events we see and hear so that we can review and inspect them later’ and that ‘People generally notice when something unexpected enters their field of view, even when they’re paying attention to something else’.Footnote84 Inattentional blindness studies reveal that the truth is significantly weirder than people tend to imagine. For Hodgson, watching other observers unknowingly experience inattentional blindness was one of the most fantastic elements of Davey’s performances. Hodgson attended five of the seventeen of the documented performances as an observer, and he wrote that ‘none of the sitters witnessed that best phenomenon, Mr. Davey writing’.Footnote85 In other words, Hodgson considered the best trick to be the fact that Davey was able to invisibly deploy many of his methods of writing the slates while in plain view of the sitters, who believed they were keenly observing him to detect trickery. Because Hodgson was expecting the trick, he was able to watch Davey openly executing his moves while the sitters failed to notice them. Today, narrative descriptions of inattentional blindness research usually begin with Mack & Rock’s seminal experiments. Occasionally, Ulric Neisser’s original (although largely unpublished) work on ‘selective looking’ will be mentioned.Footnote86 But I propose that some of the best and earliest experimental evidence for the phenomena can be found, largely unnoticed, in the Mal-Observation Report, which was published more than 100 years before the concept of inattentional blindness gained mainstream scientific acceptance.

Reconstructive Memory and the Unreliability of Eyewitness Testimony
There is now a rich psychological literature on the potential unreliability of eyewitness testimony. The concept of ‘reconstructive memory’ is now widely accepted by the scientific community. The term refers to the idea that our memories are not recordings to be played back as much as narratives that we ‘reconstruct’ each time we engage in recall; as a consequence, what we perceive as memories are not only traces of past events, but are also composed of our expectations, imaginations, and assumptions.Footnote87 Once again, this is a deeply counterintuitive concept; the process of reconstruction is very different from the generally accepted idea that memory operates analogously to recording devices like video cameras. Evidence of false memories provides significant empirical support for the theoretical framework of reconstructive memory, and researchers have now repeatedly demonstrated that people are capable of confusing imaginary events with real memories.Footnote88 A key breakthrough in this literature can be traced to research conducted in the 1970s on how leading questions can cause witnesses to mistakenly report having seen imaginary events. Loftus and Palmer demonstrated that participants could be induced to remember seeing events that were not presented in response to leading questions.Footnote89 One week after having watched a video of a car accident, participants were explicitly asked, ‘Did you see any broken glass?’ Because the video itself did not actually show any broken glass, reported false memories of broken glass were arguably induced by the question itself. These results are particularly important because they demonstrate to participants that leading questions do not merely have the potential to change people’s interpretations of their own recollections; they can reliably cause people to create false memories of entirely fictional events. Subsequent studies have demonstrated that false verbal suggestions presented cocurrently with events can also induce false reports.Footnote90 While, researchers continue to explore and debate many details, the theory of reconstructive memory is now widely accepted. But, as with inattentional blindness, conventional narratives around the research do not extend back into the 1880s, rather, they typically begin with the work of Fredrick Bartlett, who not only drew a clear distinction between reproductive and reconstructive memory, but also argued that reconstructive memory processes were more typical of how people remember events in real-world circumstances. Footnote91 While the Mal-Observation Report did not directly inspire twentieth-century psychologists, it nonetheless can be seen on of the earliest examples evidence of reconstructive memory processes, and modern anomalistic psychologists now argue that memory illusions play an important role in accounts of paranormal experiences.Footnote92

‘The Science of Magic’ And Anomalistic Psychology
Empirical investigations involving performance magic and illusion played a critical role in the establishment of Experimental Psychology as a scientific discipline, and several prominent early psychologists wrote about the psychology of magic tricks, particularly as they related to illusions of spiritualist phenomena.Footnote93 Most of these early writings represent early examples of a subdiscipline now known as anomalistic psychology. This is a branch of psychology that actively seeks naturalistic explanations for why many people report having extraordinary experiences that seem to be paranormal, i.e., outside the realm of conventional science. Christopher French has written that anomalistic psychology ‘attempts to explain paranormal and related beliefs and ostensibly paranormal experiences in terms of known (or knowable) psychological or physical factors’.Footnote94 The Mal-Observation Report represents an excellent early example of this principle, in that the researchers deliberately used naturalistic methods (i.e., conjuring/magic tricks) to help develop a better psychological understanding of how people might come to report extraordinary paranormal experiences. Notably, the power of these deceptions to falsely induce paranormal experiences has not diminished with time: In 1980, Singer and Benassi used magic trick methods to convince university students that they had witnessed a genuine demonstration of psychokinesis (aka metal bending).Footnote95 Lesaffre and colleagues, showed that contemporary undergraduate students still reliably endorsed mediumistic explanations in response to magic performances.Footnote96 Other researchers have adapted some of the same methods used by Davey to create convincing illusions of extraordinary technology, convincing participants dummy equipment is variously capable of reading their minds or controlling their thoughts; instead of pretending that the source of the extraordinary phenomena is attributable to spirits, these researchers gesture towards (fictional) neuroscientific tech and algorithms.Footnote97 These continued investigations promise to help shed new light on how people can formulate and update their beliefs in response to unusual experiences. Looking back on the Mal-Observation Report through the lens of contemporary research in anomalistic psychology, an important takeaway is how relatively minor errors of perception and memory, combined with a bit of deliberate deception and a pre-existing will to believe, can create a cascade of reasoning errors that can result in the formation of extraordinary beliefs.

Performance magic was largely ignored by the scientific community throughout the twentieth century.Footnote98 There is no definitive answer as to why twentieth−century psychologists largely disengaged with magic as a research tool; however, two potential explanations include early psychologists’ motivations to actively distance themselves from what they viewed as the pseudoscientific associations of psychical research and the prominence of behaviourism.Footnote99 Both explanations stem from researchers attempting to more firmly establish psychology as a formally recognised science. In the case of psychical research, this meant establishing clear boundaries between scientific psychology and psychical research, and magic tricks were arguably too closely associated with mediumistic fraud for researchers to be comfortable engaging with them professionally. Similarly, behaviourism, with its rejection of any type of psychological data that could not be reduced to an externally measurable behaviour, effectively rules out the study of magic and illusion, given that these experiences only occur as internal representations in people’s minds. However, throughout the past 25 years, researchers have increasingly been turning to magic trick methods as a tool for developing new paradigms to explore human cognition.Footnote100 Performance magic represents a rich, and still relatively underutilised, resource for cognitive scientists.Footnote101 The idea of adapting magic trick methods to psychology experiments has proven to be productive, with researchers developing new laboratory paradigms that help illuminate a wide variety of cognitive mechanisms, including inattentional blindness, change blindness, introspection, decision making, problem solving, memory, and amodal perception.Footnote102 Although neither Hodgson nor Davey identified themselves as psychologists, The Mal-Observation Report arguably established the first-ever framework for how to adapt magic trick methods to aid in psychological research.

Conclusion
Throughout their writing, Hodgson and Davey were careful to acknowledge that just because they could simulate mediumistic phenomena using magic trick methods, it did not necessarily follow that every testimony related to a spiritualistic experience was attributable to deception. Both men left open the possibility that scientific advancements and new experimental work might one day vindicate some of the empirical claims of proponents of Modern Spiritualism.Footnote103 To date, while discussions do continue, the mainstream scientific establishment has not come to embrace the principles of modern spiritualism in ways that were prophesized by writers like Alfred Russell Wallace and Arthur Conan Doyle.Footnote104 Nonetheless, the Mal-Observation Report represents a valuable contribution to psychological research. Many of the report’s most controversial findings about testimony, deception, attention, memory, and anomalous experience have since been borne out by subsequent formal empirical investigations related to inattentional blindness, reconstructive memory, anomalistic psychology, and the science of magic. Even though we arguably cannot contact the dead in a mediumistic sense, Hodgson and Davey’s voices live on through their writings, and can still provide valuable lessons to for contemporary readers.
