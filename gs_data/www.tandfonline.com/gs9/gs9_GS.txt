Gender, migration distance, and spousal preferences among migrant workers in China
Siyi Liu,Chu Liu &Yufeng Wu
ABSTRACT
China is experiencing a decline in marriage and birth rates. However, there is a notable absence of comprehensive research on migrant workers’ marital behaviors, especially with a focus on gender differences. Using survey data collected in 2020, this study sheds light on the interplay among gender, migration distance, and spousal preferences. Key findings reveal that compared with male migrant workers, females in China tend to favor finding spouses in their destination cities. As the migration distance expands, female migrants’ willingness to marry locals diminishes more than that of their male counterparts. Moreover, the study reveals that both male and female migrant workers who own homes in their destination cities find it easier to marry locals. However, the effect of homeownership does not significantly differ between genders, contrary to traditional expectations that men benefit more from property ownership in the marriage market. These results contribute to the broader understanding of marriage behaviors in the context of migration and urbanization in China, challenging established gender norms. Through this, it offers a fresh spatial dimension to gender studies, enriching the existing migration literature.

Introduction
In 2023, approximately 44.6% of migrant workers in China were under the age of 40 (National Bureau of Statistics of China Citation2024). Unlike the older generation of migrants, the new cohort frequently engages in multi-regional mobility as a standard aspect of their urban employment (Fan and Chen Citation2013). This heightened mobility, coupled with evolving social institutions and personal experiences, has led to significant shifts in their attitudes and practices toward marriage and relationships. Consequently, this group faces increased risks and complexities in their marital lives, making their situation a focal point for scholarly inquiry and policy consideration. Moreover, China is confronting the societal challenges of low marriage and birth rates. The marriage rate has continuously decreased from 9.9‰ in 2013 to 5.8‰ in 2020 (Yuwa Population Research Citation2022). Meanwhile, the birth population declined from 17.86 million in 2016 to 9.56 million in 2022 (National Bureau of Statistics Citation2024).

Prior research reveals two critical gaps. Initially, despite emerging trends, there is a dearth of studies on the marital behaviors of migrant workers, especially through a gender lens. Earlier, rural women working outside without their spouses were perceived as risky, primarily relegated to domestic roles and confined to rural settings (Chuang Citation2016; Fan Citation2004). However, changing societal views in China now see migration as empowering for women, enhancing their life quality and autonomy, and reducing stigma (An, Broadbent, and Yuan Citation2018). Despite these gains, full societal acceptance and transformation are pending, with married female migrants facing workplace disparities and fewer protections than their single or male counterparts. Young, single migrant women, while seemingly more independent in pursuing relationships and marriage, often face pressures from traditional gender norms that restrict their autonomy and push them toward conventional roles (Connelly, Roberts, and Zheng Citation2010; Fang Citation2012). Additionally, existing research often overlooks how varied migration experiences affect marital behaviors, tending to assume uniform migration patterns and focusing on broad trends like economic stress and social integration challenges (Mu and Yeung Citation2020; Chen and Wang Citation2015). These studies suggest migration leads to delayed marriages, increased instability, and altered marital expectations, but fail to delve into how specific migration trajectories impact these outcomes, highlighting the need for more nuanced, individual-centric research on the diverse effects of migration on marital behaviors.

This study seeks to fill these critical gaps by focusing on the marital preferences of unmarried migrant workers in China, with particular attention to the roles of gender and migration distance. Specifically, it examines how gender influences preferences for local spouses and how these preferences vary with increasing migration distance. The study also investigates the role of homeownership as a critical variable, exploring its impact on the perceived ease of marrying locally and challenging traditional assumptions about its gendered significance. By adopting a quantitative approach, this research aims to uncover the spatial and gendered dimensions of marital behaviors, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of the interplay between migration and marriage in China.

This research has several unique contributions. First, it introduces a gendered perspective to the study of migrant workers’ marital preferences, addressing the differentiated pressures and opportunities that men and women face in the marriage market. Second, it incorporates migration distance as a key variable, providing insights into how spatial mobility influences marital decisions. Third, it highlights homeownership as both a material and symbolic factor in shaping spousal preferences, offering new perspectives on how economic resources intersect with gender in the context of marriage. Finally, the study situates these findings within the broader literature on migration and urbanization, emphasizing their implications for both academic research and policy-making. For instance, understanding the gendered dynamics of marital preferences can inform policies aimed at supporting migrants’ integration into urban communities, while insights into the role of homeownership can guide housing policies to better address the needs of migrant populations.

In sum, this study examines the intersection of gender, migration distance, and marital preferences among China’s migrant workers, contributing to a deeper understanding of the social dimensions of migration. By bridging gaps in the literature and offering new empirical insights, it aims to enrich the broader discourse on gender, migration, and urbanization.

Literature review
Understanding why people get married is central to analyzing the marital behaviors of Chinese migrant workers in this study. Marriage decisions are influenced by a wide array of factors, including age, educational background, socioeconomic conditions, homeownership, gender, ethnic background, religion, children, and emotional considerations such as love and happiness (Eekelaar Citation2007; Hewitt and Baxter Citation2012; Horowitz, Graf, and Livingston Citation2019; Stutzer and Frey Citation2006). Among these, economic factors often dominate as primary motivators for marriage (Horowitz, Graf, and Livingston Citation2019). In heterosexual relationships, marriage frequently functions as an optimal economic partnership, where men and women form unions to consolidate resources and improve financial stability (Liu, Chang, and Su Citation2017). An Australian study provides further insight into the role of financial and educational factors in shaping marriage patterns, particularly in a gender-sensitive context. It finds that homeownership significantly increases the likelihood of marriage for both men and women. Additionally, men with higher socioeconomic status are more likely to transition into marriage, while highly educated women also exhibit a higher propensity to marry (Hewitt and Baxter Citation2012). These findings suggest that both financial stability and educational attainment play pivotal roles in marital decisions.

Beyond these factors, social networks also emerge as critical determinants of marital behaviors, particularly among migrant populations. The literature highlights that social networks influence marriage by providing opportunities for social interaction, creating pressures to conform to cultural or societal expectations, and facilitating introductions to potential partners (Haggerty et al. Citation2023; Valenzuela, Halpern, and Katz Citation2014). Migrant workers, who often experience separation from traditional family structures, may rely more heavily on extended social networks, such as workplace connections or community groups, to navigate marital decisions. These networks can shape individual preferences, provide emotional and logistical support, and even reinforce norms and expectations surrounding marriage.

Adding to these dynamics, China’s persistent gender imbalance in birth ratios further influences marriage patterns among migrant populations. The gender imbalance arises primarily from a deep-rooted cultural preference for males, especially in certain regions where families often lean toward having sons (Jiang and Sánchez-Barricarte Citation2012). In particular, research indicates that the central regions witness a more accentuated gender skew, influenced by intertwined economic and cultural factors (Li, Jiang, and Feldman Citation2014). Due to the relative paucity of females, females command a heightened bargaining position in the marital sphere. Such dominance extends beyond mere numbers, allowing these women more excellent selectivity and setting elevated benchmarks for potential spouses (Ebenstein and Sharygin Citation2009; Tuljapurkar, Li, and Feldman Citation1995). For example, they are often inclined to wed men of a superior socioeconomic bracket, allowing them access to enhanced living standards and resources (Bulte, Heerink, and Zhang Citation2011). Research also showed that females prefer to marry partners from an equal or, more often, superior socio-economic background (Gilmartin and Tan Citation2013). This dynamic naturally exerts considerable pressure on men, especially migrant workers transitioning from rural settings to urban landscapes. Such individuals often need help with the challenge of having comparable educational opportunities to their urban contemporaries (Du Citation2023). The following section delves into the role of gender in shaping marriage behaviors among migrant workers, exploring how economic pressures and social norms diverge for men and women in the context of migration.

Gender and marriage behavior of migrant workers
Considering the economy as a factor in migration while being gender sensitive helps reveal the multiple motives behind migration (Lutz Citation2010). Such a lens examines different genders’ perspectives on marriage and the underlying economic calculation. Different expectations for domestic roles in marriage, as part of social norms in Chinese society, diverge men’s and women’s views on marriage. Different genders, hence, seek different values in marriages. Particularly for the economic part of domestic affairs, men have traditionally been regarded as breadwinners, while women have sought bread providers. These expectations may shape migrants’ attitudes toward economic factors in marriage decisions.

As a burden
The financial implications surrounding marriage, especially in Chinese society, are profound. Traditional norms dictate substantial economic responsibilities upon the groom and his family. These responsibilities include providing a marital home, often known as the “marriage house,” and other amenities, setting the stage for significant economic pressures (Wei and Zhang Citation2011). This economic responsibility is especially burdensome for male migrant workers. Due to their often-limited income from working in urban areas (Choi and Peng Citation2016b) and rising urban property prices, many male migrant workers find it challenging to meet the conventional expectation of owning a house before marriage (Yang, Wang, and Eklund Citation2020).

Another dimension of this pressure is the historically rooted “bride price” concept. This practice involves the groom's family offering money or gifts to the bride's family (Jiang and Sánchez-Barricarte Citation2012). Over the years, the bride price has escalated in many regions, further exacerbating the economic challenges men face (Sambe, Avanger, and Agba Citation2013). Research by Zhao, Chen, and Li (Citation2023) has also shown that the social implications of not adhering to these marriage-related economic norms can be significant. Men who are unable to provide the expected material conditions may face stigmatization, lowering their overall attractiveness in the marriage market.

Furthermore, the growing commercialization of weddings, influenced partly by global trends and the desire to symbolize status and success, means even the wedding ceremony itself can be a significant financial drain (Li Citation2022). Collectively, these elements contribute to the pronounced economic stressors associated with marriage for men in China, particularly for those from less affluent backgrounds or those working in lower-paying sectors.

As an economic strategy
On the other hand, female migrants find marriage migration an attractive and attainable means to meet their settlement goal (Fan and Huang Citation1998; Gaetano Citation2008). They can treat migration as an opportunity to meet future spouses in cities, where they can “marry up” to gain upward mobility (Mu and Yeung Citation2020). Abiding by a gendered economic approach toward migration (Lutz Citation2010), the following paragraphs discuss how marriage as an economic strategy has been carried out and how it has been used as a ladder to achieve social mobility during migration in China.

Marriage as an economic strategy is not a contemporary phenomenon but has been practised throughout the decades in the new China context. The power dynamics of who gets to leverage marriage as an economic strategy changes over time and reflect a rural and urban dichotomy and the rise of female power. In the late twentieth century, women's role in marriage migration was generally passive (Song and Luke Citation2014) as the results of marriage migration largely depend on men’s decisions (Fan and Huang Citation1998). The fathers and the future husbands negotiate the decision; women have less voice in determining their marriages. Especially in rural areas, the idea of value exchange as the essence of marriage is vivid. Accordingly, helping sons get wives is considered recruiting daughters-in-law as laborers for the family (Fan and Huang Citation1998).

Coming to the 1990s1990, women's social status has been improved, and migration restriction in the hukou system has been lifted (Shen Citation1995). More rural females migrated to urban areas. These rural female migrants became the major population that wielded marriage migration: the weights and the players of different sides of the scale shift. Women became the players; they get to decide what weights to put and what weights to gain. One appealing reason for rural female migrants to do so lies in the persistent institutional constraints embedded in the hukou system. While formal migration restrictions have been gradually relaxed, the hukou system continues to structure access to social welfare, family-related benefits, and local public services in ways that disproportionately disadvantage rural women. Marrying men with urban hukou entails improving the standard of living, housing, social networks, other types of resources (Gaetano Citation2008; Qian and Qian Citation2017), and achieving social mobility promotion in terms of earning urban hukou (Dribe, Eriksson, and Scalone Citation2019; Liang Citation2016 cited in Wang and Hu Citation2019). Through their narratives, rural female migrants articulated new values associated with marriage migration. There appear to be two sets of criteria, “conditions” and “affection” (Gaetano Citation2008). While “affection” is self-explanatory, the criterion of “conditions” embodies a series of socioeconomic resource planning. Women will carefully consider their husbands’ hukou, property status, occupation, and family backgrounds, which embody social and financial status (ibid).

However, the scale is not always in favor of female migrants. There appear to be several trade-offs in conducting marriage migration. This observation generally happens between rural female migrants and local men’s marriages (Jin, Zhang, and Yang Citation2016). For women, the marriage migration may result in a high personal cost (Davin Citation2007). To obtain the urban hukou, they trade off some of men's merits, such as age, financial capability, and well-being (Fan and Huang Citation1998; Wei and Cai Citation2014). For example, they will marry local men who might be older, poorer than other urban locals, or mentally or physically disabled (Fan and Huang Citation1998). Educated rural women compromise with husbands who might have lower degrees than theirs (Qian and Qian Citation2017). Generally, the cases validate that marriage is an economic strategy for female migrants. Their main appeal in marriage is to remain in the city.

This form of marriage migration has also shifted in recent years as hukou’s effect on migration has been diminishing (Liu and Wang Citation2020; Zhu and Chen Citation2010). Migration from rural to urban areas is more accessible than before in China. The change in the hukou system is suspected to mediate females’ perception of marrying men with urban hukou as they can obtain urban hukou more easily themselves. However, more recent research is needed to validate the speculation.

Female empowerment
Migration can serve as a means of social advancement and provide opportunities for occupational and social mobility (Dribe, Eriksson, and Scalone Citation2019; Mu and Yeung Citation2020). This can be explained by the fact that migrants generally choose to go to bigger cities that can provide these opportunities. Funneling down to female migrants, it is found that they tend to marry men who have better socioeconomic status, and that the further away they are from home, the more likely they are to marry better (Dribe, Eriksson, and Scalone Citation2019). Using marriage as an economic strategy can be one explanatory factor for the correlation. In the meantime, this study also examines changes in female migrants’ perceptions as another explanation.

During migration, female migrants are encouraged to marry of their own free will. The empowerment comes from different aspects, and majorly focuses on gender equality (Connelly, Roberts, and Zheng Citation2011; Gaetano Citation2008; Li and Wang Citation2021). Living in bigger cities, being exposed to urban cultures, participating in public life, and enjoying more significant social and economic resources (Connelly, Roberts, and Zheng Citation2011), female migrants change their perception of gender equality during their stay in destination cities as they are more urbanized (Chen Citation2014). They gradually cannot accept the traditional gender norms and practices in their cities of origin and try to escape these constraints (Gaetano Citation2008). In pursuing an urban lifestyle, female migrants demand better choices and greater autonomy in marriage (Li Citation2017). They see the autonomy of courtship and marriage as a form of feminism (Gaetano Citation2008), motivating them to stay in cities instead of returning to their hometowns and giving up the power of choosing marriage.

China is not the only country where female migrants experience a change in lifestyle after migration. The literature has proposed that changing lifestyle through migration is a form of empowerment. According to the normal life theory, migrants’ accustomed perception of normal life changes throughout migration, reshaping their ideas about what a normal life should be (Bygnes and Erdal Citation2017). For example, in Poland, it is expected that females take on more roles in domestic affairs and childcare. However, in Norway, gender equality is high, and both genders share domestic and childcare responsibilities equally. The differences in everyday life motivate migrants to stay in Norway to enjoy the social norms, while Polish practices are not favored. That is to say, because of migration, migrants see different kinds of living and are empowered to choose.

Another consequence of female empowerment reflects on the diverging attitudes toward returning home. Young single female migrants who are also better educated are more likely to remain permanently in destination cities than their male counterparts (Jin, Zhang, and Yang Citation2016; Liu and Wang Citation2020; Zhu and Chen Citation2010). Male migrants, compared to female migrants, tend to return to the countryside because it is easier for them to reclaim social status and reintegrate into rural life (Choi and Peng Citation2016b; King and Lulle Citation2022).

Moreover, returning home has different meanings for male and female migrant workers. Men who return to their native towns often find their societal standing enhanced, their urban experiences positioning them as bearers of modern knowledge and, possibly, increased wealth (King and Lulle Citation2022). For women, however, such a return might entail stepping back into a more orthodox setting. Their liberties could be limited here, particularly in communities where male offspring are still highly prized (King and Lulle Citation2022). This traditional preference can shadow a woman's life decisions, compelling her to weigh the familiarity of rural existence against the allure of modern urban freedoms.

Overall, it is suggested that female migrants are empowered by migration. Through migration, their perceptions shift. They are exposed to more lifestyle paradigms and can choose which kinds of everyday life they want. The pulling effect of gender equality in host cities and the push factor in terms of lagged social norms work as a pair of forces that mediate female migrants’ settlement intention and make them see that pursuing marriage is a freedom of choice. Therefore, female migrants tend to marry men in destination cities in order not to return home.

Drawing from the detailed examination of the socioeconomic aspects surrounding marriage practices in China, especially the distinctive experiences of women in navigating the urban-rural divide, this study formulates the first hypothesis:

H1: Female migrant workers are more inclined to seek spouses locally (migration destination cities).

Marriage preference: distance as a factor
In the intricate web of marriage customs and traditions, each society possesses unique nuances influenced by its historical, sociological, and cultural background. One existing finding based on the Swedish society, which suggests that female migrants will marry better if they are further away from home (Dribe, Eriksson, and Scalone Citation2019), seems to be impractical under the lens of Chinese social norms and values. It is already shown that Chinese marital traditions, deeply rooted in ancient customs and further molded by modern socio-political policies, present a distinct pattern in which gender plays a crucial role in marriage migration decisions. In response to the correlation between marriage and distance, less is understood about how it would translate to Chinese society. This review delves into gender-sensitive aspects of how distance might affect migrants’ intentions in settlement.

Historically, it is patrilinear traditions that have influenced China's matrimonial practices, where lineage, inheritance, and family continuity are passed down through the male line (Hu and Scott Citation2016). A central aspect of this tradition is the post-marital residence pattern, predominantly characterized by women moving to their husband's family residence after marriage (Fan and Huang Citation1998; Xiong Citation2023). The move requires them to detach from their hometown, childhood memories, close-knit community, and, most poignantly, their immediate families. This distance, both physical and emotional, can be a significant deterrent. The daunting task of integrating into a new family is amplified when faced with the challenges of adapting to a new region with potentially distinct dialects, customs, and traditions. Besides, rural parents generally do not favor outsiders, or people from different provinces (Choi and Peng Citation2016b). The girls’ parents cannot see their daughters regularly and could not help if their daughters were abused (ibid). Young migrant women also find that living closer to home is a self-protection strategy as their family can act as a protector (ibid). Thus, the cultural practice of uxorilocal residence not only influences the post-marital living arrangement but also may shape unmarried female migrants’ perspectives and apprehensions regarding marrying into distant areas.

Filial piety is a core value in Chinese culture, emphasizing the importance of caring for one’s parents. This cultural expectation plays a significant role in marriage migration, as moving far away from parents can make fulfilling this responsibility more challenging. Although both male and female offspring express a solid intention to take care of their parents, in reality, women are the de facto caretakers. In a study about the impact of rural-to-urban migration on family dynamics focusing on male migrants, multiple interviewees indicate that their sisters or wives are the ones taking care of their parents (Choi and Peng Citation2016a). For example, a construction worker in Shenzhen said that it was his elder sister who took care of their sick mother; another excerpt indicates that it was the second son’s wife who took care of the son’s mother (ibid). Factoring in that women are the practical caretakers, it is reasonable to speculate that women may show a stronger intention in marrying to an urban place that is closer to their rural origins.

Moreover, China's One-Child Policy, which reshaped familial responsibilities, may strengthen this marriage pattern of female migrants. As single-child families became prevalent, the sole child, regardless of gender, was tasked with the care of ageing parents (Alpermann and Zhan Citation2019). However, women experienced unique pressures in this regard. Women traditionally joined their husband's family upon marriage, often bearing responsibilities toward their elderly relatives. Yet, being an only child meant their own parents had no other caretakers, leading to heightened responsibilities for women, especially when marrying into distant locales (Cai and Feng Citation2021). Thus, this duality of care became incredibly complex when geographical distance from natal families was introduced.

Based on the analysis above, the second hypothesis of this study is:

H2: Compared to males, the willingness of female migrant workers to marry locals decreases more significantly as the migration distance increases.

Data and methods
The data for this study were collected in 2020, focusing specifically on migrant workers in China. The survey was conducted across 46 cities with a high concentration of migrant workers, including major international hubs such as Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen, as well as coastal cities like Dongguan, Jiaxing, and Nantong. A total of 23,381 responses were received. The survey employed a stratified sampling method to ensure representation across various regions and demographic groups. Questionnaires were distributed both online and through local labor offices to maximize accessibility and response rates.

The dependent variable utilized in this investigation is termed “partner,” derived from the survey question: “Where do you expect your future partner to be from?” Respondents were presented with three distinct options to select from (1) “Local,” signifying a preference for a partner from the local area, from the current city of residence; (2) “Hometown,” indicating a desire for a partner from their place of origin; and (3) “Elsewhere,” suggesting a preference for a partner from a location other than the local area. (4) “don’t know.” Those who selected “don’t know” were excluded from this study. We interpret those who choose “local” possess an inclination for a partner hailing from the same local area. In contrast, individuals who select “hometown” or “elsewhere” express a lack of preference for a local partner. Additionally, married respondents and those who had migrated locally within the same city were also excluded. Local migrants were excluded because they are considered local residents and lack the distinct spatial and cultural dynamics that typically influence partner preferences in inter-city or inter-provincial migrations, which are central to this study. The final sample size for analysis was 2633.

The main dependent variables considered in this article include gender and migration distance. The gender variable is derived from the personal information provided by the respondents in the questionnaire. The variable “migration distance” is established by the response to the questionnaire query: “Where is your household registration (Hukou) located?” If the participant selects “Different province from the place of employment,” it will be categorized as inter-provincial migration. Opting for “Same province but different prefecture-level city from the place of employment” or “Same prefecture-level city but different county from the place of employment” will be classified as intra-provincial migration within this study. Other control variables encompass personal characteristics such as education and age, migration characteristics including migration distance, migration duration, settlement intention, employment, social network and wage level, and destination characteristics including house price and economic development (Ln (GDP per capita)) (Table 1). Migration duration refers to the length of time respondents have resided in their current location. Settlement intention indicates the respondents’ long-term intention to reside in the destination city, reflecting their desire to establish permanent roots and integrate into the local community.

Analysis
Descriptive analysis
Our initial focus is on providing descriptive evidence to test the hypotheses. In Table 2, we examine the correlation between the gender of migrant workers and their geographical preferences for a potential life partner. Among the male respondents, 370 individuals expressed a preference for a partner from elsewhere. In contrast, a significantly higher number of 1461 male respondents indicated a preference for a partner from the local destination, accounting for 79.79%. On the other hand, among the female respondents, 111 expressed a desire for a partner from elsewhere. In contrast, a notable majority of 691 females showed a preference for a partner from the local destination, accounting for 86.16%. The data reveal that female migrants are more inclined to seek life partners from the local destination than their male counterparts. This result is consistent with our H1, which suggests that female migrant workers are more inclined to seek partners locally in their destination cities.

The models utilize the respondents who expressed a desire for their future partner to come from “Elsewhere” as the reference category for comparison. Our Model 1 did not introduce any interaction terms (Table 3). The coefficient for the gender variable is positive and statistically significant, indicating that, in comparison to male migrant workers, female migrant workers are more inclined toward having local partners in the future. This result aligns with our H1. This inclination of female migrant workers toward local partners can be attributed to the various dynamics discussed earlier, such as the enhanced bargaining power they command in the marital sphere and the societal pressures males face in the competitive marriage market.

Moreover, as urbanization continues at its rapid pace, the appeal of city life and the opportunities it presents, both in terms of economic growth and personal freedom, weigh heavily on these preferences (Chen and Liu Citation2016). Women, having adapted to the urban environment, may find it challenging to give up the advantages of city life, particularly when returning to rural areas might involve surrendering certain liberties and opportunities (Chuang Citation2016). This reiterates the notion that women's decisions in the marriage market are influenced not just by economic factors but also by a desire for a broader spectrum of life opportunities and escaping the confines of traditional structures.

Regarding migration distance, the model's results indicate that compared to migration within the province, migrant workers who move outside the province are less inclined to choose a spouse at their destination. This inclination can possibly be attributed to several factors. Firstly, Chinese culture places a strong emphasis on family and community, creating deep connections with one's hometown. This makes local marriages more appealing, as they help maintain essential relationships, provide a robust support system, and foster a strong sense of belonging (Bossen Citation2007). Additionally, selecting a partner from a similar cultural background ensures a foundation of shared values, traditions, and language. This, in turn, makes communication and understanding easier within the relationship and helps avoid potential challenges arising from differences in cultural norms that might occur with partners from distant regions. Practical considerations also come into play (Bossen Citation2007). Marrying someone from the same local area is more feasible for migrant workers due to the complex logistics of managing two households over significant distances. Being geographically close allows for easy family visits, active participation in local activities, and the smooth fulfillment of familial responsibilities (Choi and Peng Citation2016a).

Briefly addressing the control variables, among the individual characteristics, both age and education are significantly related to their preferences for a spouse. Model 1 indicates that as migrants’ age increases, they tend to marry locals. This trend could be attributed to several factors: older migrants may have had more time to adapt to the local culture and social norms, as supported by Berry's (Citation1997) acculturation theory. Additionally, older individuals may place greater emphasis on career stability and social connections, as they are more likely to prioritize long-term goals over exploration and mobility (Elder, Johnson, and Crosnoe Citation2003). These factors can make local partners more appealing, as such relationships often offer a sense of stability and community integration. Regarding education, the coefficient for migrants with higher education is positive, suggesting they may be more inclined to marry locally (at their destination) compared to those with lower education, but this result is not statistically significant in Model 1. This indicates that while higher education may be associated with better adaptability to new cultures and environments (Chen and Wang Citation2015; Mu and Yeung Citation2020), such adaptability does not necessarily translate into a statistically significant preference for local partners.

Additionally, aside from migration distance, Model 1 also considers the relationship between other migration characteristic factors and spouse preferences. In terms of duration, the result shows that the longer migrant workers stay in the local area, the more inclined they are to seek future partners locally. However, migration duration exceeding 5 years does not show significance. Secondly, the model's results indicate a significant correlation between the wage level of migrant workers and spouse preferences. As the wage level increases, the coefficient is negative, indicating that respondents earning over 5000 RMB are more inclined to seek a spouse in other regions rather than locally, compared to lower-income individuals. This could be because higher-income migrant workers may have greater mobility and broader social networks, enabling them to connect with potential partners in different areas. Additionally, they might prioritize compatibility and personal preferences over proximity, given their increased financial capacity to sustain long-distance relationships or relocate if necessary. This finding suggests that higher wages may reduce the perceived constraints of geographic location in spousal preferences. Moreover, Model 1 suggests that there is no significant relationship between occupation and spouse preferences. Furthermore, migrant workers’ intention to reside long-term is significantly associated with their spouse preferences. Migrant workers who express a desire for prolonged local residency are more inclined to prefer a spouse from the local area. The results of Model 1 indicate that the social networks of migrant workers are also related to spouse preferences. Respondents who consider local connections to be necessary within their social networks show a notably higher inclination toward desiring a local spouse. This tendency may arise from their close connections to the local community, making them more inclined to seek a marital partner who shares a similar community background (Asadullah and Wahhaj Citation2019). This choice enhances their sense of integration within the society, as their spouse shares comparable social and cultural contexts, making it easier to adapt and understand each other's lifestyles and values. Consequently, their spouse preference is significantly influenced by their prioritization of local social integration. Lastly, Model 1 indicates that the local economic and housing price levels are not significantly correlated with the spouse preferences of migrant workers.

The coefficients of the interaction term were statistically significant, suggesting that the relationship between migrant’s gender and their spouse preference varies with distance (see Table 4). However, the coefficient of an interaction term in a non-linear regression is not a suitable way to understand the interaction (Mize Citation2019). It is evident that as the migration distance increases, there is no difference in spouse preferences among male migrants. However, a significant difference emerges for female migrants. Specifically, the predictive margins decrease from 0.89 to 0.79. This result further supports our H2 that, in comparison to males, the reduction in the willingness of female migrant workers to marry individuals from the local area is more pronounced as the migration distance increases.

This trend can be attributed to several intertwined factors. Historically, China's patrilinear traditions have dictated post-marital residence patterns, often requiring women to relocate to their husbands’ families. This separation from their hometowns, communities, and immediate families creates emotional and cultural challenges, making the prospect of marrying into distant locales daunting. Additionally, China's One-Child Policy has intensified responsibilities for women, who are expected to care for aging parents and in-laws. Marrying into distant areas accentuates this responsibility, as women are often required to manage the care of both sets of parents from afar. Therefore, combining cultural practices and familial obligations contributes to a greater decrease in female migrant workers’ willingness to marry locals as migration distance increases.

Homeownership and spousal preference
In China's marriage market, homeownership is not only a symbol of wealth but also a foundation for marital stability. The literature suggests that male migrant workers experience considerable pressure to acquire a home due to their greater financial responsibilities in marriage (Wei and Zhang Citation2011). On the other hand, female migrant workers often prioritize whether their potential spouse owns property, as it represents economic stability and security. Table 5 illustrates the distribution of homeownership among unmarried male and female migrant workers. The overall proportion of unmarried migrant workers who own homes is low. The homeownership rates among males (7.97%) and females (7.73%) are very similar, indicating minimal gender differences. In this section, we will examine the relationship between homeownership and marriage behaviors among migrant workers, with a particular focus on how homeownership shapes unmarried migrant workers’ subjective perceptions of the difficulty in marrying locals.

Discussion
In today's youth marriage market, the logic of the market economy has fundamentally transformed the traditional matchmaking model based on reputation and trust within close-knit communities. As Becker (Citation1981) explains in his theory of the economics of marriage, individuals make rational decisions in the marriage market, seeking partners who can maximize their economic and social utility. What was once a system built on social connections has now shifted to one in which assets, particularly property, play a central role. Bourdieu’s (Citation2011) theory of social capital further supports this shift, suggesting that, in modern economies, social capital tied to familial networks and reputation is increasingly replaced by material assets as the basis for forming relationships. Love, as the foundation of marriage, has been increasingly externalized into tangible conditions that potential partners must meet. Due to the status-symbol nature of homeownership, a woman's property in China becomes a form of capital that enhances her attractiveness in the marriage market. Owning property grants a woman bargaining power, giving her greater autonomy and agency in selecting a partner, as Oppenheimer (Citation1988) argues in her studies on how economic independence shifts traditional gender dynamics in relationships. Moreover, the fact that owning property garners more attention in the marriage market is significant, as in today’s high housing cost environment, property not only symbolizes social status and economic power but also represents future financial security and the possibility of achieving a higher quality of life. Women who own property clearly gain more decision-making power and agency in choosing a partner, with homeownership playing an empowering role in this process.

Moreover, property ownership functions as a significant bargaining tool for women within the context of marriage. The quest for equality in familial and marital relationships has been a central focus of family studies for decades (see Harris Citation2003; Rosenbluth, Steil, and Whitcomb Citation1998; Wilcox and Nock Citation2006). For unmarried women who purchase property or married women who contribute financially to the purchase of a marital home, these actions are often motivated by the desire to secure a more equitable position within the household. The resource theory provides a useful framework for interpreting these dynamics, positing that marriage is fundamentally an exchange relationship, in which each partner contributes resources to satisfy mutual needs (Aida and Falbo Citation1991). In this context, the partner with greater resources typically holds more power within the relationship. Economic resources, particularly property, play a crucial role in determining the distribution of power, as property is often the largest financial asset in a family’s portfolio. A woman’s participation in property acquisition not only signifies her economic contribution but also enhances her bargaining position within the family (Cui, Yu, and Huang Citation2023). By contributing to the purchase of a home or owning property independently, women can elevate their status within the household, granting them increased decision-making authority and influence in marital affairs. Thus, homeownership serves as a strategic tool for women to negotiate more egalitarian gender dynamics, aligning with their aspirations for greater equality in family relationships.

Conclusion
The transformation of China's societal norms and stereotypes regarding female migrant workers has indeed marked a significant step toward empowerment and improved quality of life for many women. Migration has allowed women to break free from traditional roles, gain autonomy, and experience modern living. However, this transformation is not challenging, particularly for married women and young single migrants who may face different forms of pressure and expectations. Through an extensive analysis of the 2020 survey data, this paper has elucidated the complexities and nuances of this interplay and presents the following conclusions.

First, compared with male migrant workers, female migrant workers in China tend to favor finding spouses in their destination cities. This study suggests several intertwined factors drive this trend. Firstly, traditional Chinese marriage norms place a hefty financial burden on men, such as the obligation to provide a “marriage house” and meet the “bride price” expectations (Jiang and Sánchez-Barricarte Citation2012). Many male migrants, strained by these economic responsibilities and coupled with societal stigmas for not meeting them, often find themselves at a disadvantage in the marriage market. On the other hand, women, particularly those hailing from rural areas, have historically used marriage as an economic strategy (Connelly, Roberts, and Zheng Citation2011; Gaetano Citation2008; Li and Wang Citation2021). Additionally, as these female migrants immerse themselves in urban environments, they reshape their views on gender roles and personal freedoms. The allure of the city's lifestyle and its modern liberties often overshadows the idea of reverting to the restrictive norms of their rural upbringing. Moreover, returning home poses different outcomes for the two genders. Men often regain or boost their social standing, but women might find themselves constrained by traditional norms, potentially jeopardizing the freedoms they have come to cherish.

More importantly, compared to males, the willingness of female migrant workers to marry locals decreases significantly as the migration distance increases. Traditional Chinese matrimonial practices have roots in patrilinear traditions where lineage, inheritance, and family continuity are maintained through the male line. Women typically move to their husband's family residence after marriage. Such a move necessitates detachment from their original community, childhood memories, and immediate families. Besides, women are the primary caretakers for the elderly. When the migration distance increases, this detachment becomes more profound, making it a more significant emotional and physical barrier for women. Integrating into a completely new family in a distant location, possibly with different dialects and customs, can be daunting. Hence, the farther they are from their origin, the more challenging and less appealing it becomes for them to marry locals. However, marriage remains a fundamentally different experience for males. In the traditional Chinese context, they are not expected to leave their family and community behind; instead, they live in familiar surroundings, often with the same dialect and customs. Despite this phenomenon gradually changing with the development of Chinese society, it remains mainstream (Bossen Citation2007). Moreover, the One-Child Policy, implemented in China, has had vast sociological effects. In the context of marriage and migration, one significant mediating factor will be familial responsibilities. As discussed, children are expected to take care of their aging parents, and daughters in the families are the practical caretakers. With the rise of single-child families, these sole children (irrespective of their gender) become primary caretakers for their ageing parents. However, upon marriage, women often find themselves with the dual responsibility of caring for their husband's elderly family members and their own parents. This challenge is exacerbated when the migration distance is greater, making it more difficult for women to cater to both sides of the family. The distance from their original homes may compromise the care they can provide to their parents, which may influence their marriage choices. However, males face distinct considerations in the context of the One-Child Policy and its sociological implications. In many cases, males are encouraged to focus on providing financial support to their parents, but they are not necessarily the primary caregivers (Chen, Fan, and Chu Citation2019). This difference in caregiving roles between males and females can significantly shape their attitudes toward marriage and migration.

Moreover, homeownership plays a critical role in shaping spousal preferences and marriage dynamics among migrant workers in China. The results indicate that homeownership is a significant factor for both male and female migrant workers in the local marriage market. While men traditionally face pressure to provide housing, the study reveals that women who own property experience enhanced confidence and bargaining power in selecting a spouse. This finding aligns with established theories of the marriage market, which hold that economic resources such as property elevate an individual’s status and increase their decision-making autonomy. Although the interaction term between gender and homeownership was not statistically significant, the overall results suggest that homeownership is equally important for both genders. Women who own property are empowered not only in terms of their attractiveness as potential partners but also in their ability to negotiate more equitable relationships. The results of this study challenge traditional gender norms, highlighting the shifting dynamics in marriage markets driven by economic factors like property ownership. Further research should explore the long-term effects of homeownership on marriage stability and gender equality within households.

However, this study still has some limitations. One limitation is that all respondents in our sample hold agricultural hukou, which restricts our ability to assess the impact of the hukou system on spousal preferences. This is an important structural factor that could influence marital behaviors, and its exclusion represents a constraint in this study. Another limitation is the exclusion of questions about respondents’ relationship history and current status in our initial survey. This omission limits the depth of our analysis, particularly in understanding how past and present relational experiences might influence spousal preferences. Also, because we use cross-sectional data, we cannot gain insight into respondents’ actual marital behaviors. Previous research has shown that there can be discrepancies between behavioral intentions and actual behaviors (Riva et al. Citation2011). Moreover, while settlement intention is included as a control variable in the analysis, it is possible that reciprocal causation exists between settlement intention and spousal preferences. Although the primary independent variables in this study are gender and migration distance, the potential endogeneity related to settlement intention represents a methodological limitation. Future studies could employ longitudinal data or instrumental variable approaches to address this issue more comprehensively. Lastly, although this study provides reasonable explanations for the model's results, the lack of qualitative research prevents us from delving deeper into how various factors contribute to the marital behaviors of migrant workers. This gap warrants further analysis in future studies to develop a more nuanced understanding of the topic.
